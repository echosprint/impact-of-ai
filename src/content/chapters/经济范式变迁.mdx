At the dawn of the twentieth century, 40 percent of US employment was in agriculture, compared to less than 2 percent today. Nearly half of all workers held blue-collar production and manual-labor jobs in 1960, compared to only 20 percent today. While the occupation structure of the US labor market has changed since 1980, that change has been relatively modest in historical perspective.

<Note id="f87ba" time="2025-12-19T01:37:33.547Z">
劳动市场扰动被通用目的技术的突破所驱动。蒸汽动力和电力，使农业机械化。机械化摧毁了农场工作，但也创造了工厂的工作，提高了制造业生产效率。相似的，计算机数字化制造技术1970替代了精确的生产岗位，增加了数据分析和管理技能的效率。

技术突破可能快速发生，但应用普及和劳动市场变化速度却通常是缓慢的。美国农业就业每10年降低20% 在1880-1970年间，1970年蓝领工人工作也是逐步下降的。
///
In the past, labor market disruption was fueled by breakthrough generalpurpose technologies (GPTs) like steam power and electricity, which enabled the mechanization of agriculture. Mechanization destroyed farming jobs, but it also created factory jobs by increasing labor productivity in manufacturing. Similarly, computer-based manufacturing techniques developed in the 1970s replaced precision production jobs, while also increasing the availability of digital data and the value of analytical and managerial skills. 
Although technological breakthroughs often happen suddenly, technology adoption and the pace of labor market change are often gradual. The share of employment in US agriculture fell steadily by 20 percent per decade between 1880 and 1970, and the decline in blue-collar work after 1970 was equally deliberate.
///
Deming, D.J., Ong, C. and Summers, L.H. (2025) Technological disruption in the labor market. NBER Working Paper No. 33323. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. doi: 10.3386/w33323.

<Note id="42fc1" time="2025-12-05T06:42:50.994Z">
根据预测，劳动工资将趋近于达到人类劳动效果所需的算力成本。劳动收入所占份额在GDP比重将趋近于0
///
This chapter explores the long-run implications of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) for economic growth and labor markets. AGI makes it feasible to perform all economically valuable work using compute. I distinguish between bottleneck and supplementary work—tasks that are essential versus non-essential for unhindered growth. As computational resources expand: (i) the economy automates all bottleneck work, (ii) some supplementary work may be left exclusively to humans, (iii) output becomes linear in compute and labor and its growth is driven by the expansion of compute, (iv) wages converge to the opportunity cost of computational resources required to reproduce human work, and (v) the share of labor income in GDP converges to zero.
https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34423/w34423.pdf
///
Restrepo, P. (2025) We won't be missed: work and growth in the AGI world. NBER Working Paper No. 34423. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. Available at: https://www.nber.org/papers/w34423 (Accessed: 5 December 2025).
</Note>

Automation has been accelerating but remains “so-so”
“The density of robots has roughly tripled in the United States and other rich economies since 2010” (p. 3).
“Automation has increased productivity by only about 0.3 percent per year—far below the 2–3 percent contributions many expected” (p. 4, 28–29).
Displacement is real and concentrated
“Each additional industrial robot reduces employment by about 0.4 workers in the average commuting zone” (p. 12).
“Automation accounts for 20 to 30 percent of the decline in the manufacturing employment share since 1980” (p. 13).
“Automation disproportionately reduces employment and wages of non-college workers and middle-skill routine occupations” (p. 14–15).
The missing piece: weak “reinstatement” of labor
“The reinstatement effect—creation of new labor-intensive tasks—has been much weaker since the early 1980s than in earlier periods” (p. 6, 20–22).
“The labor share of national income has declined by more than 10 percentage points in the United States and many other advanced economies since 1980” (p. 22).
Automation vs. productivity paradox explained
“Many of the tasks automated in recent decades were already being performed at low cost with human labor… This explains why automation has delivered only modest productivity gains” (p. 7, 25–26).
“Firms adopt automation technologies primarily to reduce labor costs rather than because they dramatically outperform existing methods” (p. 26).
AI and recent trends follow the same pattern
“Generative AI and other recent software innovations target cognitive and routine tasks previously performed by professionals and clerical workers… early evidence suggests rapid adoption with similar displacement dynamics” (p. 30–31).
“There is little evidence so far of a strong reinstatement effect from AI” (p. 32).
Future outlook is conditional
Pessimistic scenario: “If the reinstatement effect remains weak, the labor share could fall to 40–50 percent and real wages for most workers could stagnate or decline” (p. 34–35).
Optimistic scenario: “With the right policies, automation could contribute an additional 1 to 2 percent annual productivity growth while maintaining or increasing the labor share” (p. 36).
Policy recommendations (explicitly listed on p. 37–39)
Eliminate the tax system’s bias toward capital (e.g., accelerated depreciation).
Redirect R&D subsidies away from pure automation and toward technologies that create new complex tasks for humans.
Invest heavily in education and retraining focused on skills that are hard to automate (creativity, interpersonal interaction, complex problem-solving).
https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w31910/w31910.pdf

Restrepo, P. (2023) Automation: theory, evidence, and outlook. NBER Working Paper No. 31910. Cambridge, MA: National Bureau of Economic Research. Available at: https://www.nber.org/papers/w31910 (Accessed: 5 December 2025).

In 1965, economic historian Robert Heilbroner confidently stated that “as machines continue to invade society, duplicating greater and greater numbers of social tasks, it is human labor itself—at least, as we now think of ‘labor’—that is gradually rendered redundant” (quoted in Akst 2014, p. 2). Wassily Leontief was equally pessimistic about the implications of new machines. By drawing an analogy with the technologies of the early twentieth century that made horses redundant, in an interview1 he speculated that “Labor will become less and less important... More and more workers will be replaced by machines. I do not see that new industries can employ everybody who wants a job.
